## Context

A delivered detection frame is held in the decode worker until its result
arrives, when the runner cuts the subject tiles it wants (an empty cut still
releases). That covers every frame that reaches inference. It did not cover
the frames the runner lets go WITHOUT a result: the latest-wins queue drops
a superseded frame, a bounded inference times out, an executor throws. Those
frames kept their hold until 256 later bindings had evicted them from the
runner's own bound. On a busy always-on camera the queue drops 40% of
delivered frames — three pinned frames per second — so the worker's 8-slot
hold overflowed on three of every four admits (`holdOverflow:1029` of
`leaseAdmitted:1383` in 180 s, dev 104, 2026-09-06). Every read that came
more than about a second after its frame then failed with
`toBuffer for unknown or stale frameId`; a cold engine made that the first
seven frames of every session, and D361 and D367 each removed a way of
reaching the overflow without removing the overflow.

## Decision

The runner's per-frame cutter binding (`FrameCutterBinding`, now the real
module the spec tests) ends exactly once by one of three events: the result
(`take`, then cut), a drop (`drop`, release now), or the bound. The wrapper
calls `drop` on every release the runner reports — queue supersede, error,
timeout, success — and a binding the result path already consumed makes it a
no-op, so a success cannot release twice.

## Consequences

The hold now contains only frames whose result is genuinely pending, so its
size is bounded by in-flight inferences, not by delivery rate.
`holdOverflow` regains its meaning: a runner that stopped answering. A
remaining stale-frame error is a real fault.
